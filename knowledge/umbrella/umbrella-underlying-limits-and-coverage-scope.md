# Required Underlying Limits

Because an umbrella policy pays only after your primary policies are exhausted, insurers require you to carry minimum liability limits on those underlying policies before the umbrella will apply. These minimums vary by insurer, but they commonly require a specified level of bodily injury and property damage liability on your auto policy and a specified level of personal liability on your homeowners or renters policy. The purpose of these requirements is to make sure a substantial layer of primary coverage is in place, so the umbrella functions as true excess protection rather than as a substitute for adequate primary insurance.

If you allow an underlying policy to lapse or reduce its limits below the required minimum, the umbrella policy generally treats the missing amount as your responsibility. In practice this means you would be expected to pay the gap yourself, as though you had maintained the required underlying limit, before the umbrella contributes. Keeping every required underlying policy active and at the correct limit is therefore a condition of coverage, not merely a suggestion. Reviewing your policies together at each renewal helps ensure the underlying limits still satisfy the umbrella requirements.

# What Umbrella Insurance Covers

Umbrella insurance provides excess liability protection for many of the same events covered by your underlying liability policies. This typically includes bodily injury you cause to others, property damage you cause to others, and the legal defense costs associated with covered claims. A distinctive feature of many umbrella policies is that they can also cover certain personal injury claims that standard policies may exclude or limit, such as libel, slander, defamation, false arrest, and invasion of privacy. Defense costs are often paid in addition to the policy limit, which can be a significant benefit because litigation is expensive.

Umbrella coverage can also extend protection to liability situations that arise away from home or that your primary policies do not fully address, depending on the policy terms. For instance, some umbrella policies provide coverage for liability arising from rental properties you own or from volunteer activities, subject to the specific conditions and any required underlying coverage. Because the exact scope varies between insurers and policy forms, it is important to read the declarations and endorsements carefully and confirm which underlying policies each layer of the umbrella sits above.

# What Umbrella Insurance Does Not Cover

An umbrella policy is a liability product, so it does not pay for your own injuries or your own property damage. It will not repair your car, rebuild your home, or cover your medical bills, since those losses are handled by the property and medical coverages in your primary policies. It also does not cover business activities under a personal umbrella policy, intentional or criminal acts that you commit, or liability you assume under certain contracts. Damage that you cause on purpose is excluded, as are losses tied to professional services, which usually require separate professional liability coverage.

Other common exclusions include liability connected to certain high-risk or excluded vehicles and watercraft, workers' compensation obligations, and claims that are already excluded by the underlying policy for reasons the umbrella also recognizes. Because exclusions differ among insurers, a claim that one umbrella policy addresses may be excluded by another. When you have an unusual exposure, such as a business run from home, a boat above a certain size, or professional practice, you should ask whether a separate or specialized policy is needed rather than assuming the umbrella will respond. An honest review of your exposures with a licensed agent helps avoid gaps that only surface at claim time.
