# Death Benefit and Beneficiary Designations

The death benefit is the sum of money a life insurance policy pays out when the insured person passes away while the policy is in force. It is the central promise of any life insurance contract and is typically paid to the named beneficiaries as a tax-advantaged, lump-sum amount, though some policies allow payment through installments or an annuity option. The face amount chosen at purchase generally determines the death benefit, but the final payout can be adjusted by outstanding policy loans, unpaid premiums, or riders that increase or accelerate the benefit. Understanding how the death benefit is calculated and who receives it helps policyholders plan effectively for their dependents.

## How the Death Benefit Works

For a claim to be paid, the policy must be active and premiums must be current at the time of death. When a valid claim is filed, the insurer reviews the policy status, the cause and timing of death, and the beneficiary designation before releasing funds. The gross death benefit equals the policy face amount plus any additional benefit from riders, minus any outstanding policy loans, accrued loan interest, and unpaid premiums. In whole life and other permanent policies, the death benefit may also interact with accumulated cash value depending on the specific policy design, so policyholders should review their policy documents to understand exactly what beneficiaries will receive.

## Primary and Contingent Beneficiaries

A beneficiary is the person, people, or entity designated to receive the death benefit. A primary beneficiary is first in line to receive the payout, and a policy can name more than one primary beneficiary with a specified percentage share for each. A contingent beneficiary, sometimes called a secondary beneficiary, receives the death benefit only if no primary beneficiary is living or able to accept it at the time of the insured's death. Naming both primary and contingent beneficiaries creates a clear line of succession and helps ensure the death benefit reaches the intended recipients rather than defaulting to the insured's estate.

Beneficiary designations can be structured in different ways to control how shares pass if a beneficiary predeceases the insured. Under a per capita arrangement, the deceased beneficiary's share is redistributed among the surviving named beneficiaries, while a per stirpes arrangement passes that share down to the deceased beneficiary's own heirs. Policyholders may also name entities such as a trust, a charity, or a business as beneficiaries when that better fits their estate plan. Keeping beneficiary designations current after major life events, such as marriage, divorce, birth, or death in the family, is essential because the named beneficiary on file generally controls the payout regardless of instructions left in a will.

## Changing Beneficiaries

Beneficiary designations are not permanent and can usually be updated by the policyowner during the life of the policy, subject to the insurer's process. An important exception is an irrevocable beneficiary, whose designation cannot be changed without that beneficiary's written consent. Changes to a revocable beneficiary typically require submitting a completed beneficiary change form and passing the insurer's identity verification and authorization checks before the update takes effect. Because a change request that is not properly completed and processed may not be honored, policyholders should confirm that any update has been recorded by the insurer and keep a copy of the confirmation for their records.
