# Term Life vs. Whole Life Insurance

Life insurance policies generally fall into two broad families: term life and permanent life. Term life provides coverage for a fixed period, while permanent life is designed to remain in force for the insured's entire lifetime as long as premiums are paid. Understanding the difference helps policyholders choose coverage that matches their financial goals, budget, and the length of time their dependents will rely on that protection. Both types pay a death benefit to named beneficiaries, but they differ significantly in cost structure, duration, and whether the policy builds cash value.

## Term Life Insurance

Term life insurance covers the insured for a set number of years, commonly 10, 15, 20, or 30 years, known as the term length. If the insured passes away during the term, the policy pays the death benefit to the beneficiaries; if the insured outlives the term, coverage ends unless the policy is renewed or converted. Because term policies do not accumulate cash value and only pay out during a limited window, their premiums are typically much lower than permanent coverage for the same death benefit. This makes term life a popular choice for covering temporary needs such as a mortgage, income replacement during working years, or support until children reach adulthood.

Many term policies include a renewability feature that lets the policyholder extend coverage at the end of the term, though renewal premiums usually rise sharply because they are based on the insured's older age. Some term policies also offer a conversion option, allowing the policyholder to switch to a permanent policy without a new medical exam, often within a defined conversion period. Level term policies keep the premium and death benefit constant for the entire term, which provides predictable budgeting. Decreasing term policies, by contrast, reduce the death benefit over time and are sometimes aligned with a shrinking obligation like a mortgage balance.

## Whole Life and Permanent Insurance

Whole life insurance is the most common form of permanent coverage and is designed to last for the insured's entire life. In addition to the death benefit, whole life policies build cash value, a savings component that grows on a tax-deferred basis according to a schedule set by the policy. Premiums for whole life are generally higher than term premiums because part of each payment funds the death benefit and part contributes to the growing cash value. In exchange, the policyholder gets lifelong protection and an asset that can be borrowed against or, in some cases, surrendered for its accumulated value.

The cash value in a whole life policy can typically be accessed through a policy loan or a partial withdrawal, though outstanding loans and withdrawals reduce the death benefit if not repaid. Other permanent products, such as universal life, offer more flexible premiums and adjustable death benefits, letting policyholders vary their payments within limits. Permanent coverage is often chosen for lifelong needs like estate planning, leaving an inheritance, covering final expenses, or providing for a dependent with a lifelong disability. Because permanent policies are more complex and costly, prospective buyers should weigh the higher premium against the value of lifelong coverage and cash accumulation.

## Choosing Between the Two

The right choice depends on how long coverage is needed and how much the policyholder can afford to pay. Term life is well suited to people who want the largest possible death benefit for the lowest premium during a specific stage of life, such as raising a family or paying off a home loan. Permanent life makes sense when a person wants coverage that never expires, values the cash value component, or has estate and legacy goals that extend beyond a fixed term. Some households combine both approaches, holding a permanent base policy for lifelong needs alongside a larger term policy that covers higher obligations during peak earning years.
