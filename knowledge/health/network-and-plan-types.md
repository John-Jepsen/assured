# Provider Networks and Common Health Plan Types

Health plans work with groups of doctors, hospitals, and other providers who agree to negotiated rates. These groups are called networks, and the way a plan uses its network shapes both the member's cost and the flexibility to choose providers. Two of the most common plan structures, PPO and HMO, handle networks differently. Understanding these differences helps members estimate costs and know when a referral or extra step may be required. Actual network membership and rules vary by plan and can change over time, so members should verify a provider's status before receiving care.

## In-Network Providers

An in-network provider has a contract with the health plan and has agreed to accept the plan's negotiated rates. When a member uses an in-network provider, cost-sharing such as deductibles, coinsurance, and copays is generally lower, and the provider usually handles billing directly with the plan. Because the rates are pre-negotiated, members are typically protected from being billed for the difference between the provider's list price and the allowed amount. Staying in-network is usually the most predictable and lower-cost way to receive covered care.

## Out-of-Network Providers

An out-of-network provider does not have a contract with the plan and has not agreed to its negotiated rates. Care from these providers often carries higher cost-sharing, a separate and higher deductible, or in some plans no coverage at all. In certain situations the provider may bill the member for the difference between their charge and what the plan allows, a practice sometimes called balance billing, though some protections may apply for specific emergency situations under applicable law. Members who are considering out-of-network care should confirm in advance how their plan handles it and what their expected share of the cost would be.

## PPO Plans

A Preferred Provider Organization, or PPO, plan generally offers more flexibility in choosing providers. Members can usually see specialists without a referral and can receive some level of coverage for out-of-network care, though at a higher cost than in-network care. This flexibility often comes with higher premiums compared with more restrictive plan types. A PPO can suit members who value choice and who may want access to providers outside a single network.

## HMO Plans

A Health Maintenance Organization, or HMO, plan typically focuses care within a defined network. Members often select a primary care physician who coordinates their care and provides referrals to specialists when needed. Except for emergencies, care received outside the network is usually not covered, which makes staying in-network especially important under this type of plan. HMOs often feature lower premiums in exchange for these network and referral requirements.

## Other Plan Structures

Beyond PPO and HMO, members may encounter other arrangements such as EPO and POS plans, each blending network and referral rules in its own way. An EPO, or Exclusive Provider Organization, generally covers only in-network care but may not require referrals, while a POS, or Point of Service, plan mixes HMO-style referrals with some out-of-network coverage. The specific rules, covered providers, and costs depend entirely on the individual plan. This document describes general plan types only; members should review their own plan materials or contact their insurer to confirm how their coverage works.
