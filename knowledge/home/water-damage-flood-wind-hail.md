# Water Damage, Flood, Wind, and Hail Coverage

Water and weather cause a large share of homeowners claims, but not all water or wind damage is treated the same way by a policy. The single most important distinction for a homeowner to understand is the difference between covered water damage and excluded flood damage, because the two look similar after the fact but are handled very differently. Wind and hail add another layer, since they are usually covered but may carry special deductibles in storm-prone regions.

## Water Damage That Is Usually Covered

Standard homeowners policies generally cover sudden and accidental water damage that originates inside the home. A burst pipe, an overflowing washing machine, a ruptured water heater, or an accidental discharge from a plumbing or air conditioning system typically falls within coverage. The key words are sudden and accidental: the damage must come from an unexpected event rather than a slow leak you could have prevented. Damage caused by rain or snow entering through a covered opening, such as a wind-torn roof, is also usually covered because the underlying peril is wind.

Some water-related events sit in a gray area and often require an endorsement to be covered. Water that backs up through sewers or drains, or that overflows from a sump pump failure, is commonly excluded from the base policy but can be added through a water backup endorsement. Because this endorsement is inexpensive relative to the cost of a flooded basement cleanup, many homeowners choose to add it.

## Flood Damage Is Excluded

Flood damage is excluded from standard homeowners policies and must be insured separately. In insurance terms, a flood is generally the rising or overflowing of water onto normally dry land from an outside source, such as an overflowing river, storm surge, heavy surface runoff, or a levee failure. The defining feature is that the water comes from outside and moves in along the ground, rather than escaping from a system inside your home. This is why a basement filled by a burst pipe may be covered while a basement filled by rising groundwater during a storm is not.

Homeowners who want flood protection can buy a separate flood policy, often through a national flood program or a private flood insurer. Flood coverage typically has its own limits, its own deductible, and frequently a waiting period of about thirty days before it takes effect, so buying it well before a storm season is important. Anyone in a low-lying area, near water, or in a designated flood zone should treat flood coverage as a necessary complement to their homeowners policy.

## Distinguishing Water Damage From Flood in a Claim

When both water damage and flood are possible, adjusters look at where the water came from and how it entered the home. Water that escaped from an internal system, or rain that entered through storm-created damage, points toward a covered homeowners claim. Water that rose from the ground outside or overflowed from a body of water points toward a flood, which the homeowners policy excludes. Documenting the source with photos, timestamps, and notes about weather conditions helps the adjuster classify the loss correctly and speeds up a fair decision.

## Wind and Hail Coverage

Wind and hail are named perils covered by most homeowners policies, so damage to the roof, siding, windows, and other structures from a storm is generally payable. In regions frequently hit by hurricanes, tropical storms, or severe hail, insurers often apply a separate percentage-based deductible for these events instead of a flat dollar amount. A wind, hail, or hurricane deductible is calculated as a percentage of the dwelling limit, so a two percent deductible on a home insured for four hundred thousand dollars would be eight thousand dollars before coverage applies. Homeowners in storm-exposed areas should check their declarations page for these special deductibles, because they can be much larger than the standard deductible and change the math on whether to file a smaller claim.
