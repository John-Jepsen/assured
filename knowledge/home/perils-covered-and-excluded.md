# Perils Covered and Excluded on a Homeowners Policy

A peril is the cause of a loss, such as a fire, a windstorm, or a theft. Homeowners policies differ in how they define which perils are covered, and understanding that structure is the key to knowing whether a given event will be paid. Two broad approaches exist: named-peril coverage and open-peril coverage, and a single policy often uses one approach for the dwelling and another for personal property.

## Named Perils Versus Open Perils

A named-peril policy lists the specific causes of loss that are covered, and only those listed events trigger a payment. Common named perils include fire, lightning, windstorm, hail, explosion, theft, vandalism, falling objects, and damage from vehicles or aircraft. An open-peril policy, sometimes called all-risk, reverses the logic and covers every cause of loss except those specifically excluded in the policy language. Open-peril coverage is broader and generally preferable for the dwelling, while personal property is often written on a named-peril basis unless upgraded.

The practical difference matters most in a disputed claim. Under a named-peril policy, the burden is on you to show that the loss was caused by a listed peril. Under an open-peril policy, the burden shifts to the insurer to prove that an exclusion applies. Knowing which structure your policy uses helps you understand what documentation an adjuster may request.

## Commonly Covered Perils

Most homeowners policies cover sudden and accidental events that are outside your control. Fire and smoke damage, lightning strikes, windstorms, hail, and weight of ice and snow are typically covered. Theft, vandalism, and malicious mischief are usually included, as is accidental discharge of water from a plumbing or appliance system. Damage caused by a vehicle or aircraft striking the home, and losses from riots or civil commotion, generally fall within coverage as well.

## Common Exclusions

Every homeowners policy contains exclusions that remove certain causes of loss from coverage. Flood and earthquake are the two most significant exclusions on a standard policy, and both require separate coverage, which is discussed in dedicated documents. Other typical exclusions include damage from lack of maintenance, wear and tear, rot, mold, and insect or rodent infestation, because these are considered preventable owner responsibilities rather than sudden accidents. Losses from earth movement such as sinkholes, mudslides, and settling are commonly excluded, as are damages from war, nuclear hazard, and government action.

Intentional acts by the policyholder are never covered, and losses tied to illegal activity are excluded as well. Business activity conducted from the home may be excluded or sharply limited, and certain high-risk items or breeds may carry liability exclusions. Because exclusions vary between insurers and endorsements can add back some coverage, reviewing the declarations page and the exclusions section of your specific policy is the only reliable way to confirm what applies to you.

## Why Flood and Earthquake Are Excluded

Flood and earthquake are excluded from standard homeowners policies because they are catastrophic, geographically concentrated risks that can cause enormous simultaneous losses across a region. Insurers manage this concentration by handling these perils through specialized programs, separate policies, or endorsements with their own pricing and deductibles. Excluding them from the base policy keeps standard premiums affordable while still giving homeowners a path to buy the specific protection they need. If you live in an area exposed to either risk, you should treat separate flood or earthquake coverage as an essential addition rather than an optional extra.
