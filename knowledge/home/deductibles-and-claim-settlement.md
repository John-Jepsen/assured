# Deductibles and Claim Settlement Basis

Two policy features quietly determine how much money a homeowner actually receives after a loss: the deductible and the claim settlement basis. The deductible is the amount you pay out of pocket before coverage begins, and the settlement basis decides whether depreciation is subtracted from your payment. Together they shape both the affordability of the policy and the size of the check after a claim.

## How Deductibles Work

A deductible is the portion of a covered loss that you agree to pay yourself, with the insurer paying the remainder up to the policy limit. If you have a one thousand dollar deductible and a covered loss costs six thousand dollars to repair, the insurer pays five thousand dollars and you cover the first one thousand. Choosing a higher deductible lowers your premium because you absorb more of the small claims, while a lower deductible raises your premium in exchange for a smaller out-of-pocket cost when you file. The right choice depends on how much you could comfortably pay after a loss and how often you expect to file.

Deductibles apply per claim rather than per year, so each separate covered event carries its own deductible. Because of this, it often does not make sense to file a claim that is only slightly above your deductible, since the small payment may not justify the potential effect on your future premiums. Many homeowners reserve claims for larger losses and handle minor damage themselves.

## Flat Versus Percentage Deductibles

Most homeowners policies use a flat dollar deductible for everyday claims, such as a fixed amount of five hundred or one thousand dollars. In areas exposed to severe weather, insurers frequently add a separate percentage deductible that applies only to specific perils like wind, hail, hurricane, or earthquake. A percentage deductible is calculated against the dwelling limit, so a two percent deductible on a home insured for three hundred thousand dollars equals six thousand dollars for that type of loss. These special deductibles can be much larger than the standard flat deductible, and homeowners should check their declarations page to understand which one applies to a given event.

## Replacement Cost Versus Actual Cash Value

The settlement basis is the rule the insurer uses to calculate how much a covered loss is worth, and the two main options are replacement cost and actual cash value. Replacement cost pays what it costs today to repair or replace damaged property with new items of similar kind and quality, without deducting for age or wear. Actual cash value pays the replacement cost minus depreciation, reflecting the used condition and remaining life of the damaged property at the time of loss. The difference can be substantial for older items: a ten-year-old sofa settled at actual cash value might pay only a fraction of what a new comparable sofa costs, while replacement cost would pay for the new sofa minus the deductible.

Many policies insure the dwelling at replacement cost but insure personal property at actual cash value unless you upgrade to replacement cost contents coverage. Some replacement cost policies also pay in two stages, first issuing the depreciated actual cash value amount and then releasing the remaining depreciation once you complete the repair or replacement and submit receipts. Understanding this holdback process prevents confusion when the first payment is smaller than the total estimate.

## Choosing the Right Combination

When reviewing a homeowners policy, it helps to look at the deductible and the settlement basis together, because they jointly determine your real financial exposure. A low deductible paired with replacement cost coverage gives the most generous payouts but costs more in premium, while a high deductible with actual cash value costs less but leaves you covering more after a loss. Homeowners with older belongings or an older roof benefit especially from replacement cost coverage, since depreciation would otherwise erode their settlement. Reviewing these choices at each renewal, and adjusting them as your finances and property change, keeps your coverage aligned with what you could actually afford after a claim.
