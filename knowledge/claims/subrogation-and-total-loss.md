# Subrogation and Total Loss Explained

Two concepts often surprise policyholders during a claim: subrogation and total loss. Understanding them helps you follow why an insurer takes certain steps and how a settlement amount is reached. Neither concept changes the fact that coverage is defined by your policy; they describe how the insurer handles recovery and valuation once a covered loss is being paid.

## What Subrogation Means

Subrogation is the process by which your insurer, after paying your claim, pursues reimbursement from the party that was actually at fault for the loss. For example, if another driver caused an accident and your insurer paid to repair your vehicle, your insurer may seek to recover that amount from the at-fault driver's insurer. Subrogation keeps costs with the responsible party and can help protect premiums for policyholders as a whole.

When subrogation succeeds, you may be reimbursed for part of your deductible in proportion to what the insurer recovers. You are generally expected to cooperate with subrogation, which means not signing away the insurer's right to recover and not settling separately with the at-fault party in a way that undercuts the insurer's claim. Keeping records and responding to requests supports a successful recovery.

## How Total Loss Is Determined

A total loss occurs when the cost to repair damaged property, sometimes combined with its salvage value, meets or exceeds the property's value or a threshold set by policy or regulation. For a vehicle, this often means the repair estimate approaches the car's actual cash value. When property is declared a total loss, the insurer generally pays the value of the property rather than paying for repairs that would cost more than the item is worth.

## Actual Cash Value and Replacement Cost

Settlements are commonly calculated on one of two bases. Actual cash value pays what the property was worth at the time of the loss, which reflects depreciation for age and wear. Replacement cost pays what it costs to replace the item with a comparable new one, sometimes in two stages where depreciation is released after replacement is completed. Which basis applies depends on the policy and the type of property, and knowing the basis helps you understand why a payment is a particular amount.

## Salvage and Ownership After a Total Loss

When an insurer pays a total loss, it typically takes ownership of the damaged property, which is then sold as salvage. In some cases a policyholder may choose to keep the salvage, in which case the settlement is reduced by the salvage value and the property may carry a branded or salvage title that affects its future use and value. If you wish to retain salvaged property, tell the adjuster early so the settlement can be calculated correctly.
