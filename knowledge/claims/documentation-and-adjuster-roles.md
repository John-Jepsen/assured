# Documenting a Claim and Who Reviews It

Good documentation is the evidence that supports a claim. The adjuster uses it to confirm what happened, to measure the extent of the loss, and to determine how the policy applies. The stronger and clearer the documentation, the faster a claim can usually be resolved. You are generally expected to cooperate with reasonable requests for information, and providing what is asked promptly keeps the claim moving.

## Common Documentation Requirements

Most claims call for a written or recorded description of the loss, photographs or video of the damage, and an itemized estimate of the cost to repair or replace what was lost. Property claims often benefit from a home inventory or receipts that establish ownership and value of damaged items. Auto claims typically require photos of vehicle damage and a repair estimate. When a loss involves theft, injury, or a possible crime, an official report from police or another authority is commonly requested. Keep originals when you can and provide copies, and hold on to damaged property until the adjuster confirms it is no longer needed.

## Proof of Loss and Sworn Statements

For larger property claims, an insurer may ask for a formal proof of loss, which is a signed statement detailing the items lost, their value, and the amount claimed. This document carries legal weight, so it should be accurate and complete. Deadlines can apply to submitting a proof of loss, and missing them may affect the claim, so it is wise to note any date the insurer provides and to ask for an extension in writing if you need more time.

## What an Adjuster Does

A claims adjuster is the professional who investigates the claim, evaluates coverage, and estimates the amount of the loss. The adjuster reviews the FNOL, gathers documentation, may inspect the damage in person or by photos, and applies the policy terms to decide whether and how the loss is covered. The adjuster is not the person who invents coverage; coverage is defined by the policy, and the adjuster's job is to apply those terms consistently and fairly.

## Types of Adjusters

A staff or company adjuster is employed by the insurer and handles claims on its behalf. An independent adjuster is contracted by the insurer to handle claims in a region or during periods of high volume, such as after a storm, and represents the insurer's interests. A public adjuster is hired and paid by the policyholder to advocate for the policyholder's side of a claim, usually for a fee based on the settlement. Understanding who represents whom helps you know whose interests each person is serving during the process.

## Field, Desk, and Independent Handling

Simple claims are often handled by a desk adjuster who works remotely from photos and documents, while complex or large losses may involve a field adjuster who inspects the site. Specialized losses, such as those involving structural engineering or business finances, may bring in outside experts whose reports the adjuster relies on. Throughout, you have the right to ask questions, to understand the basis for a decision, and to request an explanation in writing.
