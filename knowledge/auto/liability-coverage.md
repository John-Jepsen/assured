# Auto Liability Coverage

Liability coverage is the foundation of nearly every auto insurance policy and is required by law in most places. It pays for harm that you cause to other people when you are at fault in a crash. Liability does not pay for your own injuries or repairs to your own vehicle; instead, it protects the other party and shields you from paying those costs out of pocket. Because it is legally mandated in most states, drivers usually cannot register or operate a vehicle without carrying at least a minimum amount.

## Bodily Injury Liability

Bodily injury liability covers the medical expenses, lost wages, and related costs of people who are hurt in an accident you cause. This includes drivers and passengers in the other vehicle, pedestrians, and sometimes passengers in your own car depending on local rules. If an injured party takes legal action, this coverage can also help pay for your legal defense and any resulting settlement or judgment, up to your policy limits. Choosing higher bodily injury limits gives you more protection when serious injuries lead to large medical bills or lawsuits.

## Property Damage Liability

Property damage liability pays to repair or replace property that you damage in an at-fault accident. The most common example is another person's vehicle, but it also applies to fences, mailboxes, storefronts, utility poles, and other structures. As with bodily injury, this coverage steps in only for damage to someone else's property, never your own. Selecting an adequate limit is important because even a single collision with a newer vehicle can exceed a low property damage limit.

## Understanding Liability Limits

Liability limits are often written as three numbers, such as 100/300/100, expressed in thousands of dollars. The first number is the most the policy pays for bodily injury to any one person, the second is the total bodily injury payout for a single accident involving multiple people, and the third is the maximum for property damage per accident. If a claim exceeds these limits, you are personally responsible for the remaining amount. Many drivers choose limits well above the state minimum to reduce the risk of paying large sums directly.

## Why State Minimums May Not Be Enough

State minimum liability limits keep you legal, but they are often modest and can be quickly exhausted by a serious accident. Modern medical care and vehicle repair costs frequently surpass the lowest allowable limits, leaving a gap that becomes your personal financial responsibility. Carrying higher limits, and in some cases an umbrella policy for added protection, is a common way to guard personal assets. Reviewing your limits periodically helps ensure your coverage still matches your financial situation and the rising cost of claims.
