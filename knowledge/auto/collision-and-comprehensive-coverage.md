# Collision and Comprehensive Coverage

Collision and comprehensive are the two coverages that pay for damage to your own vehicle, and together they are often called physical damage or "full coverage." Unlike liability, which protects other people, these coverages focus on repairing or replacing your car. They are usually optional under state law, but a lender or leasing company almost always requires them while you still owe money on the vehicle. Understanding the difference between the two helps you choose the right protection and set appropriate deductibles.

## Collision Coverage

Collision coverage pays to repair or replace your vehicle when it is damaged in an impact, regardless of who is at fault. This includes hitting another car, striking a stationary object like a guardrail or pole, and single-vehicle accidents such as rolling into a ditch. If another driver is at fault and insured, your insurer may pursue reimbursement from their carrier and refund your deductible through a process called subrogation. Collision is especially valuable for newer or financed vehicles where repair costs would be difficult to absorb yourself.

## Comprehensive Coverage

Comprehensive coverage, sometimes called "other than collision," pays for damage that does not come from a crash with another vehicle or object. Covered events typically include theft, vandalism, fire, falling objects, storms, flooding, hail, and collisions with animals such as deer. Cracked or shattered glass from a covered peril also falls under comprehensive on most policies. Because these events are often outside your control, comprehensive provides broad protection against unpredictable losses.

## How Deductibles Work

Both collision and comprehensive coverages come with a deductible, which is the amount you agree to pay before insurance contributes to a claim. For example, with a 500 dollar deductible on a 3,000 dollar repair, you pay 500 and the insurer covers the remaining 2,500. Choosing a higher deductible lowers your premium but increases your out-of-pocket cost when you file a claim, while a lower deductible does the opposite. Selecting a deductible you could comfortably pay in an emergency is a practical way to balance monthly cost against risk.

## Actual Cash Value and Total Losses

When a vehicle is damaged beyond economical repair, the insurer may declare it a total loss and pay its actual cash value rather than the cost to fix it. Actual cash value reflects what the vehicle was worth just before the loss, accounting for age, mileage, condition, and depreciation. This payout may be less than what you originally paid or still owe on a loan, which is why gap coverage exists for financed vehicles. Keeping maintenance records and understanding your car's value helps you anticipate how a total loss settlement might be calculated.
