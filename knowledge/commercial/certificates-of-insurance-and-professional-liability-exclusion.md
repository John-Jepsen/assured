# Certificates of Insurance and the Professional Liability Exclusion

A certificate of insurance is a standardized one-page document that summarizes the coverage a business carries. It lists the policies in force, the named insured, the coverage types, the limits, and the policy effective dates, and it is issued by an insurance agent or broker on the insurer's behalf. Businesses commonly request certificates from vendors, contractors, and tenants as proof that adequate insurance is in place before signing a contract or beginning work. The certificate itself does not change or extend coverage; it only reports what the underlying policies already provide.

Because a certificate is only a snapshot, it is important to understand what it can and cannot do. If the details on the certificate ever conflict with the actual policy language, the policy controls, since the certificate is informational and not a contract. A certificate holder who wants assurance that they will be notified if coverage is canceled, or that they will receive certain rights under the policy, must rely on the policy terms and endorsements rather than the certificate wording alone. Requesting a certificate is a useful first step, but verifying the underlying coverage is what actually confirms protection.

Two contract requirements frequently appear alongside a certificate request, and each has a specific meaning. Adding a party as an additional insured extends certain coverage under the policy to that party, and it must be accomplished through an endorsement to the policy, not merely by listing the party on the certificate. A waiver of subrogation prevents the insurer from later seeking recovery from the named party after paying a claim, and it likewise requires an endorsement. When a contract calls for these features, the certificate should reference the supporting endorsements so the requesting party can confirm they are actually in force.

The professional liability exclusion is a common reason a general liability policy will not respond to certain claims. Standard commercial general liability policies are designed to cover bodily injury and property damage, and they typically exclude losses that arise from the rendering of, or failure to render, professional services. This means claims tied to the quality of professional advice, design, or judgment fall outside the general liability policy, even when a client suffers a real financial harm. Recognizing this exclusion prevents a business from assuming its general liability policy covers professional mistakes when it does not.

Businesses that provide expertise or advice usually close this gap with a separate professional liability policy. Professional liability coverage, also known as errors and omissions insurance, responds to claims that a professional's work was negligent, inadequate, or caused a client financial loss. Consultants, designers, accountants, technology firms, and similar service providers rely on it to cover defense costs and settlements that the general liability policy excludes. Reviewing both policies together helps a business confirm that everyday accidents and professional mistakes are each matched to the coverage that is actually built to handle them.
