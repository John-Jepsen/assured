# Commercial General Liability Coverage Basics

Commercial general liability, often shortened to CGL, is the foundation of most business insurance programs. It protects a business against financial loss when the business is legally responsible for bodily injury or property damage that happens to someone else. A common example is a customer who slips on a wet floor inside a store, or a contractor whose work accidentally damages a client's property. General liability responds to these third-party claims by paying covered damages and, in many cases, the cost of legal defense.

A standard CGL policy is generally organized into three coverage parts. Coverage A handles bodily injury and property damage liability. Coverage B addresses personal and advertising injury, which includes offenses such as libel, slander, and copyright infringement in advertising. Coverage C provides medical payments, which can cover minor injury costs for an injured third party without the need to establish legal fault. Understanding which coverage part applies to a given situation helps a business set the right expectations about what a claim will and will not pay.

General liability coverage is written with limits that cap how much the insurer will pay. The most common structure includes a per-occurrence limit, which is the most paid for any single claim or event, and an aggregate limit, which is the most paid for all covered claims during the policy period. Some policies carry a separate products-completed operations aggregate for claims arising after a job is finished or a product is sold. Because the aggregate limit can be used up over a policy year, businesses with frequent claims should monitor how much of the limit remains.

Defense costs are an important feature of general liability policies. In many CGL forms, the insurer pays defense costs in addition to the policy limits, which means legal fees do not reduce the amount available to pay a settlement or judgment. This differs from some other liability policies where defense costs erode the limit. Because litigation can be expensive even when a business is not at fault, the duty to defend is frequently one of the most valuable parts of the coverage.

General liability does not cover every kind of loss, and recognizing its boundaries prevents misunderstandings. It generally excludes injury to the business's own employees, which is instead handled by workers compensation, and it excludes damage to the business's own property, which belongs under commercial property coverage. It also typically excludes professional errors, auto accidents, and intentional harm. When a business needs protection beyond these limits, additional policies or endorsements are used to fill the gaps rather than stretching the general liability policy to fit.
