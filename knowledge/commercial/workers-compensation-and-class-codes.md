# Workers Compensation Basics and Class Codes

Workers compensation insurance covers the cost of work-related injuries and illnesses suffered by a business's employees. When an employee is hurt on the job, this coverage pays for medical treatment, a portion of lost wages during recovery, rehabilitation services, and death benefits in the most serious cases. In most regions, businesses with employees are required by law to carry workers compensation, and the specific rules on who must be covered vary by jurisdiction. The coverage exists so that injured workers receive prompt benefits without having to prove the employer was at fault.

Workers compensation operates on a no-fault basis, which shapes how claims are handled. An injured employee generally receives benefits regardless of who caused the accident, and in exchange the employee ordinarily gives up the right to sue the employer for the injury. This trade-off is often described as the compensation bargain, and it provides certainty for both sides. Many policies also include employers liability coverage, which responds to certain lawsuits that fall outside the standard no-fault system, such as claims brought by a third party related to an employee injury.

Class codes are a central part of how workers compensation premiums are calculated. A class code is a numeric identifier assigned to a type of work based on its typical level of risk, so that a clerical office role and a roofing crew are rated very differently. Rating organizations publish these codes along with a rate for each one, usually expressed as a cost per one hundred dollars of payroll. Assigning the correct class code to each group of employees is essential, because an incorrect code can lead to overpaying for coverage or to an unexpected additional premium after an audit.

Premium is generally driven by payroll, class codes, and an experience modification factor. The insurer multiplies the payroll in each class code by that code's rate to arrive at a base premium, so businesses with larger payrolls or higher-risk work pay more. The experience modification factor, often called the mod, adjusts the premium up or down based on the business's own claims history compared with similar businesses. A business with fewer and smaller claims than expected earns a mod below one and pays less, while a poor claims history raises the mod and increases cost.

Because premiums are estimated at the start of the policy and trued up later, workers compensation policies are commonly subject to an audit. At the end of the policy period, the insurer reviews actual payroll and job classifications to confirm that the estimated figures were accurate. If the business paid more employees or shifted work into higher-risk class codes than expected, an additional premium may be owed, and if payroll came in lower, a refund may result. Keeping clear payroll records organized by class code makes this audit smoother and reduces the chance of a surprise bill.
