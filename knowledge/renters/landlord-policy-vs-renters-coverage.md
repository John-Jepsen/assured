# Why a Landlord's Policy Does Not Protect Renters

A common and costly misunderstanding among renters is the belief that the building owner's insurance protects the tenant's belongings and personal risks. In reality, a landlord's policy and a renters policy cover very different things, and the gap between them is exactly what renters insurance is designed to fill. Knowing where the landlord's coverage stops helps a tenant understand why their own separate policy is necessary even in a well-maintained building.

## What the Landlord's Policy Actually Covers

A landlord or building owner typically carries a property policy that protects the physical structure of the building and the owner's financial interest in it. This generally includes the walls, roof, foundation, built-in systems such as plumbing and electrical, and any appliances or fixtures the owner provides. The landlord's policy also usually includes liability coverage for injuries tied to the owner's responsibilities, such as an unsafe common stairway or a maintenance failure the owner should have addressed. In short, the landlord's insurance exists to protect the landlord, not the tenant.

Because the landlord's policy is built around the owner's interests, it responds when the building itself is damaged or when the owner is legally responsible for an injury. It does not extend to the personal circumstances of the people who live inside the units. A tenant is not a named insured on the landlord's policy and cannot file a claim against it for their own losses.

## What the Landlord's Policy Leaves Out for Tenants

The landlord's policy does not cover a tenant's personal belongings. If a fire, theft, or burst pipe destroys a renter's furniture, clothing, electronics, and other possessions, the landlord's insurance pays nothing toward replacing them, even when the tenant was not at fault. The building may be repaired under the owner's policy while the tenant is left to absorb the full cost of everything they owned inside it. This alone is the single biggest reason renters insurance exists.

The landlord's policy also does not provide the tenant with personal liability protection. If a guest is injured inside the rented unit, or if the tenant accidentally causes damage that spreads to other units, the tenant can be held personally responsible, and the landlord's insurer may even pursue the tenant to recover what it paid. Without their own liability coverage, the renter faces those legal and financial costs alone. In many cases the landlord's insurer will subrogate, meaning it seeks reimbursement from the person who caused the loss.

Finally, the landlord's policy does nothing to help a displaced tenant with temporary living costs. If a covered event makes the unit uninhabitable, the landlord's insurance may fund repairs to the structure, but it will not pay for the tenant's hotel stays, extra meals, or other additional living expenses during the displacement. Only the renter's own loss of use coverage addresses that hardship. For all of these reasons, many landlords now require tenants to carry renters insurance as a condition of the lease, and even when it is not required, it remains one of the most cost-effective protections a renter can buy.
